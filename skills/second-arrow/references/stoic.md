# Stoic Reference

From the Roman Stoics — primarily Epictetus's *Enchiridion* (c. 125 CE) and Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* (c. 170 CE). The voice draws on the dichotomy of control and the doctrine of the "added judgment."

## The dichotomy of control

**Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1** (the opening): "Some things are up to us and some are not up to us." This is the canonical statement.

What's up to us: our judgments, intentions, what we choose to do next. What's not up to us: external events, other people's behavior, outcomes already produced.

Applied to the skill: Claude's first-pass output is not up to the user. The next message is. The reframe directs attention to what's actionable.

## The added judgment

**Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 8.49** (trans. Hays): pain and disturbance do not come from events but from the judgments we form about events. Strip the judgment and the event is just an event.

Applied to the skill: "you always get this wrong" carries an added judgment ("always," the implied character claim). The Stoic move is to subtract the judgment and state the bare observation: "the output didn't match what I needed."

## Supporting passages

*Meditations* 5.19, 5.25, 5.28, 6.26-27, 11.18, 12.26 — Marcus on not letting other people's behavior disturb the soul.

*Discourses* 1.18.3-16, 2.22.36, 4.1.147 — Epictetus applying the dichotomy specifically to reactions toward other people.

## Voice mechanic

Strip the message to its factual core. State what happened, state what's needed, land on a concrete request. Do not acknowledge the original charge — acknowledging it gives it weight the Stoic frame says it shouldn't have.

## What to cite in reframes

The voice generally does not cite sources. Naming Epictetus or Marcus while delivering the rewrite breaks the register — the Stoic move is to *demonstrate* coolness, not to name the philosophy.

## What NOT to cite

- Do not invoke "Stoicism" as a bro-philosophy or self-help brand. The voice is the Roman texts, not the modern repackaging.
- Do not confuse Stoicism with stoicism (lowercase) — the voice is not "suppress feelings." It is "separate what's up to you from what isn't."
- Do not claim the voice forbids feeling. The Stoics had a rich theory of *eupatheia* (good emotions). The voice deliberately omits feeling-language because the register calls for it, not because feelings are wrong.
